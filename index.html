01 · Motivation

Why Football & Economy?

🏆
Galatasaray — 2000 UEFA Cup & Super Cup

Galatasaray became the first Turkish club to win a major European trophy, defeating Arsenal in the UEFA Cup Final, then beating Real Madrid in the Super Cup.

⭐
Beşiktaş — 2017/18 UCL Group Leader

Beşiktaş became the first and only Turkish club to top their Champions League group stage unbeaten — a historic feat in Turkish football history.

🥉
Turkey — 2002 World Cup 3rd Place

Turkey finished 3rd at the 2002 FIFA World Cup — the best international result in Turkish football history, coinciding with a period of relative economic stability.

Football clubs live and die by their budgets. A world-class striker, a seasoned coach, a state-of-the-art training facility — all of it costs money. And where does that money ultimately come from? In large part, from the broader economic environment a club operates in. A strong national currency means cheaper foreign transfers. Low inflation preserves purchasing power. Healthy GDP growth fuels sponsorship deals and stadium revenues.

As a lifelong football fan from Turkey, I grew up watching Galatasaray win the UEFA Cup and Beşiktaş dominate their Champions League group — achievements that felt almost mythical. But I also watched those eras fade as Turkey's economy entered turbulence. Was it just a coincidence? I wanted to find out whether a club's international success is genuinely tied to the economic health of the country it calls home.

This project puts that question to a rigorous data science test — 31 years of Turkish football and economic data, compared against England's Premier League Big 6, to understand whether the path to European glory runs through the economy.

03 · Methodology

Four-Phase Analysis Pipeline

The analysis follows a structured data science pipeline from exploration to prediction.

Step 01

EDA & Visualisation

Time-series plots of exchange rates, per-club UEFA coefficients, national team FIFA index, and the composite economic health index for both countries.

Step 02

Hypothesis Testing

Pearson r (one-tailed, α = 0.05) with 95% Fisher CI, Spearman ρ as non-parametric backup, and normality via D'Agostino K² test.

Step 03

OLS Regression

Ordinary Least Squares regressing total club UEFA points on economic index, GDP growth, and CPI inflation to quantify explanatory power (R²).

Step 04

Machine Learning

K-Means clustering (k=3, elbow method), PCA visualisation, and three supervised classifiers with 5-fold stratified cross-validation.

06 · Conclusions

What Did We Find?

This project set out to answer one question: does a country's economic performance shape how its football clubs perform on the European stage? After 31 years of data, across two countries, nine hypothesis tests, and four machine learning models, the answer is: yes — but it depends heavily on which country you are looking at.

For Turkey, the link is real and statistically significant. A healthier Turkish economy — lower inflation, stronger lira, higher GDP growth — correlates meaningfully with better UEFA results. The OLS model explains 60.9% of the variance in Turkish club performance, a remarkably strong result for a macroeconomic predictor in sport. The golden eras of Galatasaray (2000 UEFA Cup), Beşiktaş (2017/18 UCL group leader), and the national team (2002 World Cup 3rd place) all align with periods of relative stability — and the numbers confirm what the eye already suspected.

For England, the story is entirely different. The Premier League's global television deals, foreign sovereign wealth ownership, and near-total financial independence from the UK domestic economy mean that a strong pound does not translate into better UEFA results. The model predicts country of origin with 100% accuracy — confirming these two football ecosystems operate on fundamentally different economic logic.

The most striking single finding: inflation carries the strongest signal (r = −0.590). Hyperinflation, as Turkey experienced in the 1990s, systematically destroys a club's ability to recruit and retain talent. When prices double every year, a transfer budget agreed in January is worth half as much by July. No economic factor erodes football competitiveness faster.

08 · Limitations & Future Work

What Could Be Better?

  • N = 64 per country is below ideal for ML. Combined N = 128 partially addresses this (flagged by instructor Neşe Hoca).
  • GDP and CPI are annual figures assigned to both half-years of the same year, reducing temporal granularity.
  • Per-club half-year UEFA points are estimates reconstructed from archival data; should be verified against primary UEFA sources.
  • Confounders not captured: manager changes, transfer policy, club ownership changes, and individual player quality.
  • Future work could incorporate Transfermarkt squad value data — a direct monetary measure of club investment.
  • Extending to Germany, Spain, and France would enable a true multi-country panel analysis with much larger N.
